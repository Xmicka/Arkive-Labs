Whether or not a business has decided to "adopt AI", its people are already using it. They draft emails in ChatGPT, summarise documents in Claude, and clean up spreadsheets with whatever tool is open in the browser. Most of this is genuinely useful. Some of it means customer data, contracts and internal plans are being pasted into tools nobody has checked.

A short AI usage policy lets your team keep the benefits while avoiding the obvious risks. Here is how to write one that people will actually follow.

## Start from how people use AI today

Before writing rules, ask. A five-minute anonymous survey or a team conversation will tell you which tools people use, for what, and what they would like to use more. You will usually find drafting, summarising, research, coding help and image generation. Writing a policy that bans everything people rely on just drives use out of sight.

## Section 1: Approved tools and accounts

List the AI tools that are approved for business use, and how they must be accessed. The key distinction is between **consumer accounts** and **business accounts**. Business and team plans from the major providers typically offer stronger commitments on how your data is handled, including whether your inputs are used to train models, plus admin controls and the ability to remove a user when they leave. Check each provider's current terms before approving it.

A simple rule:

> "Use only the AI tools listed in the approved tools register, signed in with your company account. Ask [the IT owner] before using any other AI tool for work."

Include a way to request new tools. People will find new tools faster than any policy can list them.

## Section 2: What data can and cannot go in

This is the most important section. Tie it to the way you already classify information. If you do not have a classification scheme, a simple three-level one works:

- **Public:** information already published. Fine to use with approved tools.
- **Internal:** internal documents, plans and general business information. Use only with approved tools on company accounts.
- **Confidential:** customer personal data, financial records, contracts, passwords, security details, anything under a confidentiality agreement. Do not enter into AI tools unless that specific tool and use has been explicitly approved for confidential data.

Spell out examples, because people do not think in categories:

> "Do not paste customer lists, CVs, payroll data, contracts, passwords, API keys or source code containing secrets into AI tools."

If your business handles personal data, remember data protection law applies to what goes into AI tools too. Our [guide to Sri Lanka's PDPA](/blog/sri-lanka-personal-data-protection-act-guide-for-small-businesses) covers the principles.

## Section 3: Check before you use the output

AI tools produce fluent text that is sometimes wrong. The policy should make one person responsible for every output used in the business:

- **Facts, figures, names and references must be checked** against a reliable source before being sent or published. Our guide on [fact-checking AI-written content](/blog/how-to-fact-check-ai-written-content) gives a simple process.
- **Code must be reviewed and tested** like any other code, including for security issues.
- **Legal, financial, HR and medical content** must be reviewed by a qualified person.

The rule in one line: "You are responsible for anything you send or publish, however it was drafted."

## Section 4: Clients and transparency

Decide how you will handle AI use in client work. Options range from noting AI-assisted work in your terms to agreeing case by case. Check client contracts too: some prohibit sharing their information with third-party tools, which includes AI services. When in doubt, ask the client.

## Section 5: Intellectual property and originality

Remind staff not to present AI output as original research, not to use it to copy a competitor's protected work, and to check image and content licences in the tools you use. Ownership and copyright of AI-generated material is still developing in many countries, so treat important creative assets with extra care.

## Section 6: AI tools connected to company systems

Newer AI tools can connect to email, calendars, drives and other apps, or act on your behalf. These need more care than a chat window, because they can read and change real data. Require approval before connecting any AI tool to company accounts, and grant only the access it needs.

## Section 7: Ownership, training and review

Name the person who owns the policy and the approved tools register. Brief the team when the policy launches, add it to onboarding, and review it every six months, because the tools change that fast.

## Keep it short

Two pages is plenty. A structure that works:

1. Purpose and scope
2. Approved tools and accounts
3. Data rules, with examples
4. Checking outputs
5. Clients and transparency
6. Intellectual property
7. Connected AI tools
8. Roles, exceptions and review

If you already have an acceptable use policy, you can add the AI rules as a section there rather than creating a separate document.

## A starting rule set

If you do nothing else this week, send your team these four rules:

1. Use approved AI tools with your company account only.
2. Never put confidential or personal data into AI tools unless that use has been approved.
3. Check every fact, figure and line of code before you use it.
4. Ask before connecting an AI tool to company systems.

That covers most of the real risk while letting your team keep working faster.
