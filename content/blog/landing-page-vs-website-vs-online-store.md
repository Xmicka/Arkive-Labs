One of the first questions we ask a new client is not "what should the site look like?" It is "how does a customer go from hearing about you to paying you?" The answer usually decides what to build. Many businesses pay for more site than they need. A few build too little and lose sales they never see.

Here is how we think about the three most common options, and how to choose between them.

## The three options, plainly

**A landing page** is a single page with one job: get a specific visitor to take a specific action. Book a call, join a waitlist, download a guide, buy one product. It has no menu of other pages to wander into. Everything on it supports that one action.

**A business website** is a set of pages that explains who you are, what you offer, why you can be trusted and how to get in touch. Home, services, about, contact, often a blog or case studies. It serves several kinds of visitors with several questions.

**An online store** lets customers browse products, add them to a cart and pay without talking to you. It needs product pages, a cart, checkout, payment processing, delivery rules, stock tracking and order emails.

## Start with how your customers buy

The right answer follows the buying journey.

**If customers buy after a conversation**, for example consulting, renovations, wedding photography, B2B services or anything priced individually, then the site's job is to earn the conversation. You need trust and clarity more than a checkout. A business website, or even a strong landing page at the start, is usually right.

**If customers buy without needing to talk to you**, for example clothing, cosmetics, books or food products with fixed prices, then every extra step between "I want this" and "paid" loses sales. An online store earns its cost here.

**If you are testing one offer**, for example a new service, an event, a course or a launch, a landing page is often the fastest and cheapest way to find out whether people want it before you build anything bigger.

## When a landing page is the right call

Choose a landing page when:

- You have **one offer** and **one audience** right now.
- Most of your visitors will arrive from **one source**, such as an ad campaign, an Instagram bio link or a QR code on packaging.
- You want to **measure** one thing clearly: how many visitors took the action.
- You need something live **quickly**, while the bigger picture is still forming.

A landing page is not a "cheap website". It is a different tool. Because there is only one path, it is easier to test headlines, offers and layouts and see which version performs better.

Its limits are real, though. A single page will not rank in search for many different topics, and it cannot answer every question a cautious buyer has. If your sales depend on people researching you over weeks, one page will feel thin.

## When you need a full website

Choose a multi-page website when:

- You offer **several services** to **different kinds of customers**, and each needs its own explanation.
- Buyers **compare you with competitors** before getting in touch, so trust signals matter: about page, team, process, examples of work, clear pricing guidance.
- You want **search traffic** over time. Each service page and article is a chance to be found for a different search.
- You need **supporting pages** that a serious business is expected to have: privacy policy, terms, contact details and a real address.

A good business website is organised around the questions buyers ask, in the order they ask them. What do you do? Is it for someone like me? Can I trust you? What will it cost and how does it work? How do I start?

## When you need an online store

Choose e-commerce when:

- Your products have **fixed prices** and customers can decide without speaking to you.
- You expect **enough orders** that handling each one by message would slow you down.
- You are ready for the **operations** behind the store: product photos and descriptions, stock, delivery, returns and customer service.

That last point is where most store projects struggle. The website is the easy part. Keeping fifty product pages accurate, photographing new stock and answering delivery questions every day is the real work. If you are not ready for that, start smaller.

There is a sensible middle step many Sri Lankan businesses use: a catalogue website where customers browse products and then order by WhatsApp or phone. It lets you test demand and learn which products sell before investing in full checkout, payment gateways and delivery logic.

## Questions to decide in ten minutes

Answer these honestly:

1. **Can a customer decide to buy without talking to you?** Yes points to a store. No points to a website or landing page.
2. **How many distinct offers do you have right now?** One points to a landing page. Several points to a website.
3. **Where will most visitors come from in the next six months?** Paid ads to one offer suits a landing page. Search, referrals and word of mouth suit a website.
4. **How long do buyers research before getting in touch?** Days or weeks of research needs a website with depth.
5. **Who will update it every week?** If nobody, keep it small. An out-of-date store or a blog with one post from two years ago hurts trust more than a simple, current site.

## Common mistakes we see

**Building a store before proving demand.** A full store is a significant investment in money and daily effort. Selling through a simple page and messages first shows you what sells.

**A website that talks about the business instead of the buyer.** Pages full of "we are passionate about excellence" answer none of the buyer's real questions. Lead with what you do, who it is for and what happens next.

**A landing page with five different calls to action.** If the page asks visitors to book, call, follow on Instagram, read the blog and subscribe, most will do none of them. Pick one.

**Forgetting the basics that build trust.** Real contact details, a privacy policy, clear prices or price ranges, and evidence of real work. These matter as much as design.

## Growing from one to the next

These options are not permanent choices. A common, healthy path looks like this:

1. Start with a **landing page** for your main offer and learn what messaging works.
2. Grow into a **website** when you add services, want search traffic, and buyers need more depth.
3. Add a **store** when repeat, fixed-price sales are frequent enough that taking orders by message is slowing you down.

If you build on a platform that can grow with you, each step reuses the work of the last one instead of starting again.

The best site is not the biggest one. It is the one that matches how your customers already want to buy, and that you can keep current.
