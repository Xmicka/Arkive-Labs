# How to Price a YouTube Script

Pricing your first scripts feels like guessing. You look at what others charge, pick something lower, and hope. A better approach is to price from your own work. It takes about ten minutes, and it gives you a number you can defend.

I don't quote market rates here. They vary widely, I haven't verified any, and the right figure depends on your costs. Instead, here's the method.

## Step 1: Count the real work

A script isn't just typing. For a documentary-style script, my work looks like this:

1. **Research.** Finding and reading sources.
2. **Fact-checking.** Confirming every claim against a source.
3. **Drafting.** Writing the script.
4. **Editing.** Cutting and reworking for the voiceover.
5. **Revisions.** At least one round of changes.
6. **Admin.** Messages with the client, formatting, delivery.

Estimate the hours for each step for a script of a given length, for example 5 minutes of finished video. Be honest, and add time for the parts you tend to underestimate. Research and fact-checking usually take longer than drafting.

## Step 2: Find your floor

Your floor is the lowest price you'd say yes to. Work it out like this:

```
Floor = (hours x the hourly amount you need) + costs + a buffer
```

Fill in your own numbers. The hourly amount should cover your living costs, time between projects, and any tax you owe. Don't borrow someone else's. If you can't comfortably take the work at your floor, don't take it.

## Step 3: Price per finished minute, then add tiers

Clients think in video length, so I express the price per finished minute and then build packages.

Fiverr invites you to offer three levels, and this is how I'd structure them for scripts:

- **Basic:** A shorter script with the essentials.
- **Standard:** A longer script with a hook written carefully and one round of revisions.
- **Premium:** The longest script, with the sourced fact list so the client can check everything.

The source list is the extra that costs the most time, so it belongs in the highest tier. If a feature costs you hours, price it.

## Step 4: Decide what's not included

Say it in writing: voiceover recording, editing the video, thumbnails, extra revision rounds, rush delivery. Each is a separate price or a clear no.

## Step 5: Check it against a few real quotes

Look at what other sellers offer for similar work, as a sanity check and not as a target. If your floor is far above what everyone charges, you either need to speed up, narrow the work, or find clients who value what you add. If you are far below, check that you haven't forgotten a step.

## Step 6: Review after each job

Track how long a script actually took. After a few, compare it with the estimate and adjust. Your first price is a draft.

## What not to do

- **Don't price from fear.** A very low price attracts demanding clients and leaves you resenting the work.
- **Don't promise unlimited revisions.** State the number.
- **Don't hide the extras.** If sourcing facts is part of your value, say so.
- **Don't copy a competitor's number without checking your own costs.**

## The takeaway

Price from your own time and costs, build tiers around the work that takes you longest, and review the number after every job. A price you can explain is easier to say out loud, and easier to defend.
