Sooner or later a customer's security questionnaire, an insurer or an auditor asks: "Can you send us your information security policies?" Many small businesses respond by downloading a 40-page enterprise template, changing the company name, and filing it. Nobody reads it, nobody follows it, and the first time someone asks a detailed question, it falls apart.

A good policy set for a small business is short, specific and realistic. This guide covers which policies you need, how to write them, and how to make them stick.

## What a policy is for

A policy is a set of rules the organisation commits to follow, approved by management. It is not a description of good intentions, and it is not a technical procedure. "Staff must use two-factor authentication for all business email accounts" is a policy rule. Step-by-step instructions for setting it up belong in a separate how-to guide.

If you are working towards ISO/IEC 27001, clause 5.2 requires top management to establish an information security policy, and Annex A control 5.1 expects topic-specific policies that are approved, communicated and reviewed. Even without certification, the same structure gives customers and insurers what they are looking for.

## The eight policies most small businesses need

1. **Information security policy.** The top-level commitment: why security matters to the business, who is responsible, and how the other policies fit together.
2. **Acceptable use policy.** What staff may and may not do with company devices, email, internet and, increasingly, public AI tools. A clear rule such as "do not paste customer or confidential data into public AI tools unless the tool is approved" prevents a common new leak.
3. **Access control policy.** How access is granted, reviewed and removed, including the rule that leavers lose access on their last day.
4. **Password and authentication policy.** Minimum password length, use of a password manager, and where two-factor authentication is required.
5. **Backup and recovery policy.** What is backed up, how often, where copies are kept, and how often restores are tested.
6. **Remote working and mobile device policy.** Rules for working from home and on the move: screen locks, encryption, public Wi-Fi, lost devices.
7. **Data classification and handling policy.** A simple scheme (for example public, internal, confidential) and what each level means for storing, sharing and disposing of information.
8. **Security incident reporting policy.** What counts as an incident, how quickly staff must report it, and to whom. The most important rule here is that people report early without fear of blame.

## How to structure each policy

Each policy should fit on about two pages. A consistent structure makes them easy to read and easy to audit:

- **Document control:** owner, version, approval date and next review date.
- **Purpose:** one or two sentences on why the policy exists.
- **Scope:** who and what it applies to.
- **Rules:** numbered, so they can be referenced ("see access control policy, rule 4").
- **Roles:** who is responsible for what.
- **Exceptions:** how to request one, and who can approve it.
- **Review:** how often the policy is reviewed.

Numbered rules matter more than they look. When an auditor or customer asks "how do you handle leavers?", you can point to a specific rule rather than a paragraph.

## Write rules you will actually meet

The fastest way to fail an audit is to write rules your business does not follow. If the policy says "all laptops are encrypted" and one is not, that is a finding. Before you approve a rule, check that it is true today or that you have a plan to make it true.

Choose values deliberately. Instead of copying "passwords must be changed every 30 days", decide what you will enforce. Current guidance from bodies such as the UK's National Cyber Security Centre favours long passwords, a password manager and two-factor authentication over frequent forced changes. Pick a minimum length, the accounts that require two-factor authentication, and the number of working days within which access is removed for leavers, and write those exact values in.

## Watch the legal points

Some rules touch employment and privacy law: monitoring of staff devices and email, use of personal devices for work, and how quickly breaches must be reported. These depend on your country and sector. In Sri Lanka, for example, the Personal Data Protection Act affects how personal data is handled and how breaches are dealt with. Have a qualified person check this wording before you approve it.

## Roll them out properly

A policy nobody has read protects nobody. A simple rollout looks like this:

1. **Tailor** each policy: replace placeholders, remove rules that do not apply, and add anything specific to how you work.
2. **Approve** them formally. A director's sign-off and a date is enough.
3. **Publish** them where staff can find them, such as a shared folder or intranet page.
4. **Brief** the team in a 20-minute session that covers the handful of rules that affect daily work most.
5. **Record acknowledgement.** Ask each person to confirm they have read the acceptable use policy and the incident reporting policy.
6. **Add them to onboarding,** so every new starter sees them in the first week.

## Keep a policy register

A one-page register listing every policy, its owner, version, approval date and next review date turns policy management into a quick monthly check. Review each policy at least once a year, and sooner if something changes: a new system, a new way of working or a security incident that showed a gap.

## What policies will not do

Policies set expectations. They do not make you secure on their own, and a policy set is not the same as a full information security management system. You still need the controls the rules describe, a risk assessment to decide which controls matter most, and evidence that the rules are followed.

Start with the eight policies above, keep them short and true, and you will have a baseline that stands up to a customer questionnaire, satisfies most insurers, and gives your team clear rules they can actually follow.
