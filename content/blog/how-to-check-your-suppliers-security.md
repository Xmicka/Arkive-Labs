Your suppliers hold your data and keep your business running. Your accounting software provider, your payroll bureau, the agency running your email list, the developer with access to your website: if any of them is breached, it can become your incident. That is why customers, insurers and auditors increasingly ask, "How do you check your suppliers?"

Large companies buy vendor-risk platforms for this. A small business does not need one. It needs a simple, repeatable process that focuses effort on the suppliers that matter.

## Why supplier security matters

Attackers often target a smaller supplier to reach its customers. And many data leaks are not hacks at all, but a supplier storing your customers' data carelessly. If you work towards ISO/IEC 27001, Annex A includes a group of controls on supplier relationships (5.19 to 5.23) covering how you manage security with suppliers, what goes into agreements, the ICT supply chain, monitoring suppliers and the use of cloud services. Even without certification, the same thinking protects you.

## Step 1: List your suppliers

Start with a register. For each supplier, record:

- what they provide
- what data they hold or can access (none, internal, confidential, personal data)
- how much you depend on them (could you work for a week without them?)
- who in your business owns the relationship

Do not forget the less obvious ones: the IT contractor with admin access, the cloud storage everyone uses, the marketing tool that holds your customer list. Most small businesses find twenty to sixty suppliers once they look properly.

## Step 2: Tier them by risk

Not every supplier deserves the same scrutiny. Assessing your stationery supplier as closely as your payroll provider wastes everyone's time. Use the two facts you recorded, the data they handle and how dependent you are on them, to put each supplier in a tier:

- **Tier 1 (high):** handles personal or confidential data, or the business would stop without them. Assess in detail and review every year.
- **Tier 2 (medium):** limited sensitive data or moderate dependence. Lighter assessment, review every two years.
- **Tier 3 (low):** no sensitive data and easy to replace. Record them and move on.

Tiering is what makes the process manageable. Most effort goes on a handful of Tier 1 suppliers.

## Step 3: Ask consistent questions

For Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers, use one questionnaire for everyone, so answers can be compared. Keep it focused, around thirty questions, across these areas:

- **Governance:** Do they have a named person responsible for security? Do they hold a certification such as ISO/IEC 27001, or an independent audit report such as SOC 2?
- **Access control:** Is two-factor authentication enforced? How is staff access removed when people leave?
- **Data protection:** Where is your data stored? Is it encrypted? Who can see it?
- **Operations:** Are systems patched promptly? Are backups taken and tested?
- **Incidents:** Will they tell you if your data is affected, and how quickly?
- **Continuity:** What happens to your service if they have an outage?
- **People:** Are staff trained in security?
- **Legal:** Do they use subcontractors that will access your data?

Score answers simply. For example, "yes" earns the full weight of a question, "partly" earns half, "no" earns nothing, and "not applicable" is left out. Weight the questions that matter most for your business more heavily.

## Step 4: Treat the score as a prompt, not a verdict

A score helps you see where to look. It does not make the decision for you. A supplier with a low score on one critical question, such as no two-factor authentication on systems holding your customer data, may be a bigger concern than one with a slightly lower total.

For each Tier 1 supplier, decide one of three outcomes: accept, accept with conditions (they fix a specific gap by a date), or look for an alternative. Record the decision and who made it.

## When a big provider will not fill in your form

Large providers such as major cloud platforms and payment processors will rarely complete a small customer's questionnaire. That is normal. Instead:

- Check their **trust or security centre**, where they usually publish certifications, audit reports and security documentation.
- Download or request their **ISO/IEC 27001 certificate** or **SOC 2 report**, and check the scope covers the service you use.
- Read their **data processing terms** to confirm where data is stored and how incidents are notified.

Record what you checked and when. That evidence is often worth more than a questionnaire answer.

## Step 5: Put it in the contract

Assessment is only half the job. For Tier 1 suppliers, make sure your agreement covers security expectations, confidentiality, how and when they must notify you of incidents affecting your data, what happens to your data when the contract ends, and, where personal data is involved, the obligations the law places on them. In Sri Lanka, the Personal Data Protection Act is relevant whenever a supplier processes personal data on your behalf. Have contract wording checked by a qualified person.

## Step 6: Review on a schedule

Add a "next review" date to every supplier in the register based on its tier, and check monthly for anything overdue. Also review a supplier whenever something changes: they suffer a breach, change ownership, or you start sending them more sensitive data.

## When a supplier leaves

Offboarding is where supplier risk is most often forgotten. When a relationship ends, remove their access, collect or confirm deletion of your data, and update the register.

## Start small

You do not need to assess every supplier this month. Build the register, tier everyone, and assess your top five Tier 1 suppliers properly. That is a credible, evidence-backed answer the next time a customer or auditor asks how you manage supplier security.
