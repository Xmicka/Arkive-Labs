In most small offices, the Wi-Fi router was installed by the internet provider years ago, still has the password printed on the sticker, and has been shared with every visitor, delivery driver and former employee since. It sits in a corner and nobody thinks about it until the internet goes down.

That router connects every laptop, phone, printer and payment terminal in the business to the internet. If someone can get onto your network or into the router's settings, they can see traffic, attack other devices and change how your internet works. The good news is that securing it takes about an hour, and most of the steps only need doing once.

## Step 1: Find out what you have

Before changing anything, gather the basics:

- **The router's make and model**, usually on a label on the device.
- **Who manages it.** Is it supplied and managed by your internet provider, or did you buy it yourselves? Some provider-managed routers limit which settings you can change.
- **How to reach the admin page.** This is usually a web address or IP address printed on the label or in the manual, such as 192.168.1.1.
- **Any other network equipment**, such as extra access points, range extenders or a separate firewall.

If an IT provider set up your network, ask them for this information and for a short written summary of the setup.

## Step 2: Change the default admin password

Every router has an admin login for its settings page, separate from the Wi-Fi password. Many still use a default login that is printed on the device or easy to find online.

1. Log in to the admin page.
2. Change the admin password to a long, unique password and store it in your password manager. Our guide to [setting up a password manager](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-password-manager-for-a-small-team) explains how.
3. If the router allows it, change the admin username as well.
4. Turn off remote administration, sometimes called remote management or WAN access, unless your IT provider genuinely needs it. This stops the settings page being reachable from the internet.

## Step 3: Use strong Wi-Fi encryption and a new password

In the wireless settings:

- **Choose WPA3** if your router and devices support it. If some older devices cannot connect, use the WPA2/WPA3 mixed mode, or WPA2 with AES as a minimum.
- **Never use WEP or an open network** for business devices. These offer little or no protection.
- **Set a new Wi-Fi password**, long and hard to guess. A string of several unrelated words works well and is easier to type than random characters.
- **Change the network name (SSID)** from the default. Avoid names that reveal the router model or make it obvious which business it belongs to if you would rather not advertise it.

Changing the password disconnects every device, so do it at a quiet time and reconnect business devices one by one. This also gives you a chance to see which devices were connected.

## Step 4: Separate guests and gadgets from business devices

Most modern routers can run a separate guest network. Use it.

- **Guest network** for visitors, customers and staff personal phones. It should give internet access only, with no access to business devices. Look for a setting that isolates guest devices from the main network and from each other.
- **Main network** for business laptops, desktops and printers only.
- **A separate network for smart devices** if your router supports it, such as CCTV systems, smart TVs and other connected gadgets, which often receive fewer security updates.

Change the guest password regularly, for example every few months, and display it only where guests need it, not on the router itself.

Card payment terminals and point-of-sale systems deserve special care. Follow your payment provider's guidance on how they should be connected, and keep them off the guest network.

## Step 5: Turn off features you do not need

Some router features make life convenient but add risk:

- **WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup)**, the push-button or PIN connection feature, has a history of security weaknesses in its PIN mode. Turn it off unless you have a specific need.
- **UPnP (Universal Plug and Play)** lets devices open connections through your router automatically. It can be abused by malware. Turn it off unless something you rely on needs it, and check that it still works afterwards.
- **Port forwarding rules** that nobody remembers setting up. Review them and remove any that are not needed. Ask your IT provider about any you do not recognise before deleting them.

## Step 6: Keep the router updated

Router manufacturers release firmware updates that fix security problems.

- Check the admin page for a firmware update option, and turn on automatic updates if available.
- If your internet provider manages the router, ask them how updates are handled.
- Find out whether the manufacturer still supports your model. Routers that no longer receive security updates should be replaced. The manufacturer's website usually lists support status.

Put a reminder in the calendar to check for updates every three months if automatic updates are not available.

## Step 7: Check what is connected

Most routers show a list of connected devices. Look through it:

- Do you recognise every device?
- Are there devices on the main network that should be on the guest network?
- Are there old devices that should be removed?

Give business devices clear names where your router allows it, so unfamiliar ones stand out. Repeat this check every few months and after anyone leaves the business.

## Step 8: Protect the router physically and write it down

- **Place the router somewhere secure**, not in a public area where anyone could press the reset button or plug in a cable.
- **Disable or cover unused network ports** in public spaces if possible.
- **Record the setup**: router model, admin login location, network names, which devices belong where, firmware update date and who to call for support. Store it with your other IT records.

## A one-hour checklist

- Admin password changed and stored securely
- Remote administration turned off
- WPA3 or WPA2-AES with a new, strong Wi-Fi password
- Guest network turned on and isolated
- WPS and unnecessary UPnP turned off
- Firmware updated, with automatic updates on where available
- Connected devices reviewed
- Setup documented

## When to get help

If your office has several access points, CCTV, a server, card terminals or staff working remotely into the office, the network is more complex and worth reviewing with an IT professional. The same applies if you are working towards ISO/IEC 27001, where network security controls and records of how they are managed will be part of your audit.

For most small offices and shops, though, an hour with this checklist closes the most common gaps. Your router will go back to sitting quietly in the corner, but it will be doing its job properly.
