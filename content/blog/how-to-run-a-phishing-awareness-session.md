Most successful attacks on small businesses start with a message: an email with a fake login link, an invoice with changed bank details, a WhatsApp from "the director" asking for an urgent payment. Technology filters out a lot of these, but some always get through. What happens next depends on the person who receives it.

You do not need a training platform to make a real difference. A well-run 45-minute session, repeated once or twice a year, changes behaviour. Here is a plan.

## Before the session

**Collect real examples.** The best material is phishing your own business has received. Ask the team to forward suspicious messages for a couple of weeks before the session, and remove any personal details before showing them. Real examples feel relevant in a way stock examples never do.

**Agree the reporting process.** Decide exactly how people should report a suspicious message: a "report phishing" button in your email system, forwarding to a named address, or a message to a named person. The session is pointless if people do not know what to do afterwards.

**Brief management.** Leaders should attend, and should agree in advance on the no-blame rule below.

## The session plan (45 minutes)

### 1. Why it matters (5 minutes)

Keep it short and concrete. Explain that criminals target small businesses because they are less likely to have dedicated security staff, and that one message can lead to stolen money, locked files or leaked customer data. One real story, from your industry or region, lands better than statistics.

### 2. How phishing works (10 minutes)

Explain the goal behind most phishing messages. Usually the attacker wants one of four things:

- your **password**, through a fake login page
- your **money**, through a fake invoice or payment request
- **access to your computer**, through a malicious attachment or link
- **information** they can use for a later, more convincing attack

Then show the warning signs:

- **Urgency or pressure:** "pay today", "your account will be closed", "I'm in a meeting, just do it".
- **A sender that does not quite match:** a lookalike domain, or a real name with an unfamiliar address.
- **Unexpected requests:** an attachment you did not expect, a login you did not start, a "shared document" from someone who never shares documents.
- **Links that go somewhere else:** hover over a link (or press and hold on a phone) to see the real address before clicking.
- **Requests to change payment details.**

### 3. The scams that cost most (10 minutes)

Spend time on the attacks that do the most financial damage to small businesses:

- **Fake invoices and changed bank details.** A supplier's email account is compromised, or a lookalike address is used, and an invoice arrives with "our new bank details". The rule: **any change to payment details is confirmed by phone, using a number you already have, never the one in the email.**
- **Impersonating the boss.** A message that appears to come from a director asks for an urgent transfer or gift cards. The rule: **unusual payment requests are verified through a second channel, however senior the person seems.**
- **SMS and WhatsApp scams.** Fake delivery notices, bank alerts and "verification codes". The rule: **never share a code you receive, with anyone.**

### 4. Practice (12 minutes)

Show six to eight real or realistic messages, one at a time, and ask the group: "Phishing or genuine? What made you decide?" Mix in a couple of genuine messages, because you want people to think, not assume everything is a trap. This discussion is where the learning happens.

### 5. What to do (8 minutes)

End with three clear actions:

1. **If unsure, do not click, reply or pay.** Check with the sender through a channel you trust.
2. **Report it** using the agreed process, even if you are not sure.
3. **If you already clicked, entered a password or made a payment, report it immediately.** Speed matters more than anything else.

Then state the **no-blame rule** out loud, ideally from a manager: "Nobody will be in trouble for reporting a mistake. The only real problem is a mistake that is not reported." People hide mistakes when they fear blame, and hidden incidents are always worse. Our guide to [the first hour of a security incident](/blog/what-to-do-in-the-first-hour-of-a-security-incident) covers what happens after someone reports.

## After the session

- **Send a one-page summary** with the warning signs, the payment-change rule and the reporting process.
- **Thank people publicly when they report,** including false alarms. Reporting is the behaviour you want to encourage.
- **Add it to onboarding,** so new starters hear it in their first week.
- **Repeat it every six to twelve months,** with fresh examples.

## Should you run simulated phishing tests?

Simulated phishing emails can show whether training is working, but use them carefully. Tell staff that simulations will happen, never use them to shame or punish individuals, and avoid cruel lures such as fake bonus or redundancy announcements. Measure **how many people report** a simulation, not only how many click. A rising reporting rate is the best sign of a healthy security culture.

## Back it up with technology

Training works best alongside controls that limit the damage when someone does click: [two-factor authentication](/blog/how-to-roll-out-two-factor-authentication-to-a-small-team) on every account, email authentication on your domain using [SPF, DKIM and DMARC](/blog/how-to-set-up-spf-dkim-and-dmarc), and a payment process that always verifies changes by phone.

Forty-five minutes, twice a year, with real examples and a blame-free reporting culture. It is one of the cheapest and most effective security investments a small business can make.
