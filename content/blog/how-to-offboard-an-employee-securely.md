When someone leaves a small business, the focus is usually on handover and farewell drinks. Access is an afterthought. Months later, a former staff member can still open the shared drive, the old agency still has admin on the website, and nobody remembers who knows the social media password.

Most of the time nothing bad happens. But leftover access is one of the most common findings in security audits, and when it does go wrong, it goes badly wrong. A simple, repeatable checklist fixes it.

## Before the last day

**1. Make a list of everything they can access.** This is much easier if you keep an access register, but if you do not, build one now for this person:

- email and calendar
- shared drives and document folders
- business apps: CRM, accounting, project tools, design tools
- website, hosting, domain and social media admin
- shared accounts and passwords they know
- company devices: laptop, phone, security keys
- physical access: keys, alarm codes, building passes
- supplier and bank portals where they are a named user

**2. Plan the handover of work and ownership.** Identify files, documents and recurring tasks they own, and who will take them over. In many tools, deleting an account can delete the files it owns, so transfer ownership first.

**3. Agree the timing with HR or management.** For most departures, access ends at the close of the last working day. For sensitive or difficult departures, access may need to end at the moment the person is told, before they leave the meeting.

## On the last day

**4. Disable accounts, do not delete them yet.** Disabling stops sign-in immediately while keeping data, emails and audit history. Delete later, after the data has been moved and any retention period has passed.

**5. Sign them out everywhere.** Disabling an account does not always end sessions that are already open on phones and laptops. Use your platform's option to sign the user out of all sessions and revoke app passwords and tokens.

**6. Remove their 2FA methods and devices,** and remove any phones or computers registered to their account.

**7. Change shared passwords they knew.** This is the step most often missed. Any shared login, Wi-Fi password, alarm code or safe combination they knew should be changed. A team password manager makes this much easier, because you can see exactly which shared credentials they had access to.

**8. Collect company equipment.** Laptop, phone, security keys, access cards, physical keys. Record what was returned.

**9. Handle their mailbox.** Common options are forwarding their email to a manager for a set period, or setting an automatic reply that names a new contact. Avoid keeping the account active indefinitely just to receive mail.

## Within the first week

**10. Remove access from every remaining app.** Work through your list. If your tools use single sign-on through Google or Microsoft, disabling the main account removes access to many of them at once, but check apps that use separate logins.

**11. Check for things they set up personally.** Admin rights they granted themselves, API keys, automation rules, forwarding rules, scheduled reports sent to a personal address, integrations connected through their account. These keep running quietly after the person has gone.

**12. Update external contacts.** Tell suppliers, banks and key customers if the person was a named contact or authorised signatory. Update domain registrar and hosting contact details if they were listed.

**13. Wipe and reissue devices.** Back up anything the business needs, then wipe company laptops and phones before giving them to someone else.

**14. Record what was done.** A short note with the date of each step is your evidence for customers, insurers and auditors, and it means the next departure is quicker.

## After the retention period

**15. Delete or archive the account and data** according to your retention rules. Personal data about the former employee, such as HR records, should be kept only as long as there is a lawful need. In Sri Lanka, the Personal Data Protection Act applies to employee data as well as customer data.

## Contractors, agencies and freelancers too

The same checklist applies when a contractor, agency or freelancer finishes working with you. They are often the access people forget: the developer who still has admin on your website two years later, or the agency that still owns your ad account. Make sure business-critical accounts are owned by the business, with outside parties added as users, not the other way round.

## Make it repeatable

Turn the steps above into a checklist template and use it for every departure. Assign each step an owner, usually HR, the manager and whoever manages IT. Review your access register every few months too, because people move roles and keep access they no longer need.

## Why this matters for compliance

If you are working towards ISO/IEC 27001, or answering security questionnaires from customers, you will be asked how you remove access when people leave, and auditors often sample recent leavers to check. A documented checklist and records of completed offboarding are exactly the evidence they look for. Our guide to [running an internal audit](/blog/how-to-run-your-first-iso-27001-internal-audit) shows how this is typically tested.

Offboarding done well takes less than an hour per person. Offboarding done badly can leave a door open for years.
