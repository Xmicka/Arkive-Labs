In many small businesses, passwords live in a spreadsheet called "logins", a note on the owner's phone, a WhatsApp group or someone's memory. Everyone uses the same few passwords across several accounts, and when a person leaves, nobody is quite sure which logins they knew.

A business password manager fixes most of this in one move. It stores every login in an encrypted vault, generates strong unique passwords, fills them in automatically and lets you share access with the right people without revealing the password itself. This guide covers how to choose one and roll it out without chaos.

## Why a password manager matters

Most account takeovers do not involve clever hacking. They rely on passwords that are reused, guessed or leaked from another website. When one site is breached, criminals try the same email and password on email accounts, banking portals and business tools. If your staff reuse passwords, one breach anywhere can become a breach of your business.

A password manager makes unique passwords practical. Nobody needs to remember forty different long passwords. They remember one strong master password, protect it with two-factor authentication, and the manager handles the rest.

It also gives you control. You can see which shared logins exist, who can use them, and remove access in one place when someone leaves.

## Step 1: Choose a business plan, not a personal one

Several well-established password managers offer business or team plans, including Bitwarden, 1Password, Keeper and Dashlane. Compare current plans and prices on their own websites, as both change regularly. Whatever you pick, look for:

- **Business ownership of the vault**, so company logins stay with the company when people leave.
- **Shared folders or collections** with permissions, so the marketing team sees marketing logins and nobody else.
- **Admin controls**: requiring two-factor authentication, setting master password rules and removing users.
- **Account recovery** for the business, so a forgotten master password does not lock away company logins forever.
- **Apps for every device** your team uses, including browser extensions and phone apps.
- **An activity log**, useful for audits and investigations.

Avoid using the password storage built into a single browser as your team solution. It is convenient for individuals but generally lacks the sharing, permissions and offboarding controls a business needs.

## Step 2: Set up the admin side first

Before inviting anyone:

1. **Create the business account** using a company email address, not a personal one.
2. **Appoint two administrators**, so you are not locked out if one person is unavailable.
3. **Turn on two-factor authentication** for both admins, ideally with an authenticator app or security key.
4. **Set up account recovery** as the provider recommends, and store any recovery codes somewhere safe and offline.
5. **Create your folder structure.** Keep it simple, for example: Finance, Marketing, Website and IT, Operations, Management only.
6. **Set policies**: require two-factor authentication for all users, and a minimum master password length.

## Step 3: Gather and clean up your shared logins

This is where most of the value comes from. Collect every shared login you can find: the spreadsheet, notes, the WhatsApp group, the sticky note on the office monitor. For each one, decide:

- **Is it still needed?** Close or delete accounts nobody uses.
- **Should it be shared at all?** Where a service allows individual user accounts, give people their own logins instead of sharing one. It is safer and shows who did what.
- **Who needs it?** Put it in the folder for that group only.

As you move each login into the vault, **change the password** to a new long, random one generated by the manager. The old passwords have been sitting in spreadsheets and chats, so assume they are no longer secret.

Once everything is moved, delete the old spreadsheet, notes and messages. Check that copies have not been left in email attachments or downloaded files.

## Step 4: Roll it out to the team

A short, planned rollout works better than an email saying "please start using this".

**Week one:**
- Hold a 30-minute session. Show how to install the browser extension and phone app, save a login, fill it in and generate a new password.
- Help each person set up their master password and two-factor authentication on the spot.
- Explain the rules simply: work logins go in the vault, never in spreadsheets or chats.

**Week two:**
- Ask everyone to move their own work logins into their vault and change any reused passwords as they go, starting with email.
- Be available to help. Most problems are small: an extension not filling a field, or a login saved twice.

A good master password is long and memorable, such as several unrelated words joined together. It must not be used anywhere else. Encourage people to write it down only if they keep it somewhere genuinely secure at home, never on their desk.

## Step 5: Make it part of how you work

The password manager only helps if it stays the single place for logins. Build it into your routines:

- **New starters** get a password manager account on day one, with access to the folders their role needs.
- **Leavers** are removed on their last day, and you change any shared passwords they could see. The password manager shows you exactly which ones those are.
- **New services** go straight into the vault when the account is created, with a generated password.
- **Quarterly checks**: review who has access to each folder and look at the manager's security report for weak or reused passwords.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Storing the master password in the vault it unlocks.** People do this. Make sure it is memorised or stored securely elsewhere.
- **Skipping two-factor authentication on the password manager.** It protects everything else, so it needs the strongest protection.
- **Putting everything in one shared folder** that the whole team can see.
- **Leaving the old spreadsheet "just in case".** It defeats the purpose.
- **Only the owner using it.** The value comes from everyone using it consistently.

## Where this fits with your other security basics

A password manager works best alongside two-factor authentication on email, banking, your website and other important accounts. Together they block most of the account takeover attempts small businesses face. Our [website security checklist](/blog/website-security-checklist-for-small-businesses) shows which website, hosting and domain accounts to prioritise.

If you are working towards ISO/IEC 27001, a password manager also gives you clear evidence for controls around authentication information and access rights: who has access to what, and proof that access is removed when people leave.

Set aside an afternoon for the admin setup and a couple of weeks for the rollout. After that, logging in is faster than it was before, and your business is far harder to break into.
