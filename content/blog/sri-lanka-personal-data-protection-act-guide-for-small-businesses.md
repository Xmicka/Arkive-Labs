Sri Lanka passed the Personal Data Protection Act, No. 9 of 2022, making it one of the first countries in South Asia with a comprehensive data protection law. Many small business owners have heard of it but are not sure whether it applies to them or what it asks them to do.

This guide explains the law in plain English, from the point of view of a small business that collects customer names, phone numbers and emails. I work on compliance and security at Arkive Labs, and these are the questions clients ask us most.

**Please read this first.** This article is general information, not legal advice. The Act is being brought into force in stages, it has been amended since 2022, and the Data Protection Authority issues rules and guidance that add detail. Before you rely on any date or requirement, check the current position with the Data Protection Authority of Sri Lanka or a qualified lawyer.

## What counts as personal data

Personal data is any information that relates to a person who can be identified from it, directly or indirectly. For a typical small business that includes:

- names, phone numbers, email and postal addresses
- WhatsApp chats with customers
- order history and delivery details
- photos and CCTV footage of identifiable people
- employee records, payslips and CVs
- IP addresses and other identifiers collected by your website

The Act also defines **special categories** of personal data that need extra care, such as health information, biometric data, data about children and information revealing religious beliefs, political opinions or racial origin. If your business handles any of these (a clinic, a gym recording health conditions, a school), you have stricter obligations.

## Who the Act applies to

In broad terms, the Act applies to processing of personal data that takes place wholly or partly in Sri Lanka. It can also apply to organisations outside Sri Lanka that offer goods or services to people in Sri Lanka or monitor their behaviour.

"Processing" is defined widely. Collecting, storing, using, sharing and deleting data all count. So a Colombo bakery that keeps a list of customer phone numbers for delivery orders is processing personal data.

Purely personal or household use, such as your own family contacts, falls outside the Act.

The law uses two important terms:

- **Controller**: the business that decides why and how personal data is processed. If you collect customer details for your shop, you are the controller.
- **Processor**: a business that processes data on a controller's behalf, such as a payroll provider, a delivery company or a marketing agency running your email list.

## The principles in practice

The Act sets out obligations that will feel familiar if you know Europe's GDPR. Here is what they mean for a small business.

**Have a lawful reason.** You need a valid basis for processing, such as the person's consent, performing a contract with them (you need their address to deliver their order), a legal obligation, or a legitimate interest that does not override their rights. Do not assume consent covers everything.

**Use data only for the purpose you collected it for.** If a customer gave you their number to receive a delivery update, adding them to a promotional broadcast list is a different purpose. Ask first.

**Collect only what you need.** If your enquiry form asks for a national identity card number "just in case", remove the field. Every extra piece of data is extra risk.

**Keep it accurate.** Make it easy for people to correct their details.

**Do not keep it forever.** Decide how long you keep each type of data and delete it after that. Old spreadsheets of former customers are a common, avoidable risk.

**Keep it secure.** Take appropriate technical and organisational measures: strong passwords, two-factor authentication, limited access, encrypted devices, secure backups. Our [website security checklist](/blog/website-security-checklist-for-small-businesses) covers the website side of this.

**Be transparent.** Tell people what you collect, why, who you share it with and how long you keep it. For most businesses that means a clear privacy notice on the website and a short explanation at the point of collection.

**Be accountable.** You should be able to show how you comply, not just say it.

## The rights your customers have

The Act gives individuals rights over their data, including the right to:

- **access** the personal data you hold about them
- **withdraw consent** where processing relies on consent
- **have inaccurate or incomplete data corrected**
- **have data erased** in certain circumstances
- **request a review of decisions made purely by automated processing** that significantly affect them

For a small business, the practical requirement is simple: know where customer data lives, so you can find it, correct it or delete it when someone asks, and respond within the time the law allows.

## Data protection officers

The Act requires some controllers to appoint a **data protection officer**, for example public authorities and organisations whose core activities involve large-scale processing, special categories of data, or regular and systematic monitoring of people. Many very small businesses will not fall into these groups, but check against the current rules rather than assuming. Even where it is not required, naming one person responsible for data protection is good practice.

## Sharing data and sending it abroad

**Processors.** If an outside company handles personal data for you, put a written agreement in place that says what they may do with it and how they must protect it.

**Cross-border transfers.** Most small businesses use cloud tools hosted outside Sri Lanka: Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Shopify, Mailchimp and many others. The Act regulates transfers of personal data outside the country and sets conditions for them. Keep a list of the tools you use and where they store data, and follow the Authority's guidance on what is required for transfers.

## Data breaches

If personal data is lost, stolen or exposed, the Act requires controllers to notify the Data Protection Authority, and in some cases the affected people, within the time and manner the rules specify. Being prepared matters more than the exact wording: know who will decide what happened, who will contact the Authority and how you will tell customers.

## Penalties

The Data Protection Authority can direct organisations to comply and can impose financial penalties for non-compliance, with higher penalties for repeated failures. The figures are large enough for a small business to take seriously, but the more realistic risk is losing customer trust after a careless leak.

## A first-steps checklist

You do not need a large compliance project to make real progress. Work through this list.

1. **Map your data.** Write down what personal data you collect, where it is stored (spreadsheets, WhatsApp, email, website, POS system, cloud tools) and who can access it.
2. **Cut what you do not need.** Remove unnecessary form fields and delete old records you have no reason to keep.
3. **Write a privacy notice.** Explain what you collect, why, who you share it with, how long you keep it and how people can exercise their rights. Put it on your website and link it wherever you collect data.
4. **Review consent for marketing.** Separate "we need this to deliver your order" from "we would like to send you offers", and record when people opt in.
5. **Secure the basics.** Two-factor authentication on email and business accounts, unique passwords, limited access, encrypted laptops and phones, off-site backups.
6. **List your processors and tools.** Note where each stores data and put agreements in place with the ones that handle customer data for you.
7. **Prepare for requests and breaches.** Decide who answers data requests and who leads if something goes wrong.
8. **Name a responsible person.** Even if you do not need a formal data protection officer, someone should own this.
9. **Check the current rules.** Visit the Data Protection Authority's website for commencement dates, rules and guidance, and revisit them every few months.

Good data protection is mostly good housekeeping: knowing what you hold, keeping only what you need, and protecting it properly. Businesses that do this early find that compliance becomes a small, routine task rather than a crisis.
