# Why Blockbuster Lost to Netflix: What the Public Record Shows

The story is usually told in one line: Blockbuster had the chance to buy Netflix, laughed, and was wiped out. It is a good story. It is also incomplete.

This post sticks to what a published report supports. The main source is CNBC's 2020 article on the rivalry, [How Netflix almost lost the movie rental wars to Blockbuster](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/09/22/how-netflix-almost-lost-the-movie-rental-wars-to-blockbuster.html). Where I add something from general knowledge, I say so.

## The offer

According to that report, Netflix co-founders Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph offered to sell the company to Blockbuster for $50 million in 2000. Hastings pitched Netflix as the online arm Blockbuster did not have. Blockbuster turned the offer down. A former Netflix finance chief later said, in a 2008 interview, that Blockbuster essentially laughed them out of the room.

That is the part everyone remembers. It is only the start.

## The money hiding in the cash register

Here is the detail that gets less attention. The same report says Blockbuster collected about $800 million in late fees in 2000, at a dollar a day for late returns.

Netflix's early pitch was simple: a flat monthly rate and no late fees. The report notes that much of its early growth came from people who were tired of paying those charges.

That puts Blockbuster in a hard spot. Competing with Netflix meant giving up a large and reliable source of income. When Blockbuster did push into online rental, it dropped late fees, and the report says that cost it hundreds of millions of dollars. Activist investor Carl Icahn and the board opposed the move.

## Blockbuster's best move, and what happened next

In 2006, Blockbuster launched a program called Total Access. Subscribers to its online service could return DVDs to a Blockbuster store and get another one for free. According to the report, Netflix could not match that.

For a while it worked. In one quarter of 2007, the report says Netflix lost 55,000 subscribers compared with the previous quarter, while Blockbuster's subscriber base kept growing. Randolph said it got "very scary" for Netflix once Blockbuster put serious resources behind its digital business.

But each in-store exchange cost Blockbuster roughly $2. And Icahn, who had opposed the earlier changes, later said Total Access "might have helped Blockbuster fend off Netflix."

Blockbuster eventually filed for bankruptcy in 2010. In the same year, under a new chief executive, the report says it brought late fees back.

## What this suggests

The record points to something more interesting than "Blockbuster was stupid":

- **Blockbuster's best income was also its biggest weakness.** Late fees were profitable, and customers resented them.
- **Blockbuster did try to compete.** Total Access shows a real attempt, and the report says it briefly worked.
- **The fight inside the company mattered.** The decision to drop late fees divided the board and the investors.
- **The $50 million offer is a symbol, not the whole cause.** Buying Netflix would not have solved the late-fee problem by itself.

These are my readings of the report, not facts it states. Other historians weigh the causes differently, and I would encourage you to read the original and decide.

## What I did not verify

I used one main source for the numbers. The bankruptcy date and the general outline are widely reported, but I have not cited a second source for them in this post. If you use this material in a video or article, check each figure against the CNBC report and at least one more outlet.

## The takeaway

A business story that fits in one sentence is usually missing something. The useful question is not "who was foolish?" but "what was each side unable to give up?"
