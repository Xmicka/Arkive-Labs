When a logo, a campaign or a website disappoints, people usually blame the designer. In our experience the problem more often starts earlier, in the brief. "We want something modern and clean that stands out" gives a creative team almost nothing to work with. They guess, you react, and a project that should take three rounds takes eight.

A good brief does not need to be long. It needs to answer the questions the team will otherwise have to guess. This is the structure we ask clients to use, and the one we use ourselves when we brief photographers, illustrators and developers.

## Why briefs go wrong

Most weak briefs fail in one of four ways.

- **They describe the output, not the problem.** "We need a new website" says what, not why. Is the current site not converting? Does it look out of date next to competitors? Are you launching a new service? Each problem leads to a different site.
- **They try to say everything.** If the brief lists ten key messages, the work will communicate none of them clearly.
- **They use words that mean different things to different people.** "Premium", "bold" and "minimal" are interpreted in very different ways. Examples beat adjectives.
- **They leave out the constraints.** Budget, deadline, brand rules and who signs off all shape the work. Hiding them leads to surprises late in the project.

## What a useful brief contains

### 1. Background

Two or three short paragraphs on the business. What you sell, to whom, how long you have been operating, and what has changed recently that makes this project necessary now.

### 2. The problem or objective

The single most important section. State what is not working or what you want to achieve, and how you will know it worked.

Weak: "Increase brand awareness."

Stronger: "Most of our enquiries come from people who already know us. We want new customers in Kandy and Galle to discover us, and we will judge this by enquiries from those areas over the next three months."

### 3. The audience

Describe the people you need to reach as specifically as you can. Not "everyone aged 25 to 45", but who they are, what they care about, what they currently do instead of buying from you, and what makes them hesitate. If you have real customer quotes, include them. A sentence a real customer said is worth a page of demographics.

### 4. The one thing to communicate

If the audience remembers only one thing, what should it be? Write it in one sentence. Then list up to three supporting points that make it believable. Everything else is optional.

### 5. Deliverables

List exactly what you need, with formats and sizes where you know them. "Logo" is vague. "Primary logo, a simplified version for social profile images, and files for print and web" is clear. If you are not sure what you need, say so, and ask the team to recommend.

### 6. Mandatories and brand rules

Anything that must or must not happen: existing brand guidelines, colours, fonts, legal text, product names, languages (English, Sinhala, Tamil), accessibility needs, platforms the work must run on.

### 7. References, with reasons

Share examples you like and dislike, and explain why in one line each. "We like the calm spacing on this site" is useful. A list of links with no comment invites the team to copy the wrong thing.

### 8. Budget, timeline and decision-makers

Give a realistic budget range, the real deadline and any fixed dates (a launch event, a trade fair). Name who approves the work. Few things slow a project more than a decision-maker who first sees the work at the final round.

### 9. How success will be measured

Decide in advance what "good" means. Sometimes it is a number: enquiries, sign-ups, sales. Sometimes it is a decision: "the board approves the new identity". Agreeing on this up front turns feedback from taste into judgement.

## A template you can copy

```
Project name:
Date / version:

1. Background (what we do, for whom, what changed):
2. Problem or objective (and how we will measure it):
3. Audience (who, what they care about, what makes them hesitate):
4. The one thing to communicate:
   Supporting points (max 3):
5. Deliverables (list, formats, sizes):
6. Mandatories (brand rules, languages, legal, platforms):
7. References we like / dislike, and why:
8. Budget range:
   Deadline and fixed dates:
   Who approves:
9. What success looks like:
```

## A short example

**This example is fictional.** It shows the level of detail that helps, not a real client.

> **Background:** A family-run spice company in Matale selling packaged spice blends to supermarkets for twelve years. Supermarket margins are falling, so they want to sell directly to customers online.
>
> **Objective:** Launch a direct-to-customer range. Success in the first three months means 150 online orders and a repeat-purchase rate we can measure.
>
> **Audience:** Home cooks in Colombo and Sri Lankans living abroad who miss familiar flavours. They worry that packaged spices are stale or mixed with fillers.
>
> **The one thing:** These are the same small-batch blends the family has used for three generations, packed within a week of grinding.
> Supporting points: grind date printed on every pack; no fillers or added colour; delivered island-wide.
>
> **Deliverables:** Packaging for six blends, a product photography direction, and a landing page for the launch.
>
> **Mandatories:** Keep the existing logo. Labels in English and Sinhala. Pack sizes are fixed by the current filling machine.
>
> **References:** We like the honest, ingredient-first labels of a European tea brand we will share. We dislike glossy "luxury" spice packaging that hides the product.
>
> **Budget, timing, approvals:** Budget range agreed separately. Packaging must reach the printer six weeks before the launch date. The founder and her son approve together.
>
> **Success:** 150 orders in three months, and at least a quarter of first-time customers ordering again.

Notice what the example does: it names a real tension (staleness and fillers), one clear message, and a measurable goal. A team can make good decisions from this.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Writing the brief after the kick-off call.** Write it first, then use the call to fill gaps.
- **Changing the objective mid-project.** If the goal changes, update the brief and agree on what that means for time and budget.
- **Letting the brief go stale.** Treat it as a living document. Date each version.
- **Briefing by committee without a final owner.** Gather input widely, but one person should own the brief.

## How to use it once it is written

Send the brief before the first meeting, and ask the team to come back with questions rather than ideas. Their questions show you what is still unclear. Then, at every review, hold the work up against the brief. "Does this communicate the one thing to this audience?" is a far more useful conversation than "do we like it?"

The time you spend writing a clear brief is the cheapest time in any creative project. It is the difference between a team guessing what you want and a team solving your actual problem.
