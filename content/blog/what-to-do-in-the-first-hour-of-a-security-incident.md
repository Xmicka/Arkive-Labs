Security incidents rarely start with alarms. They start with a strange email from a colleague's account, a customer asking why they received an odd invoice, files that will not open, or a login alert from another country. What a small business does in the first hour often decides whether the incident is a bad afternoon or a bad month.

This is the checklist I give clients. Print it, and keep it somewhere you can find it when your systems may not be working.

## Before anything else: stay calm and start writing

Open a notebook or a document on a device you trust, and write down the time and what was noticed. Keep adding to it: every action, who did it and when. Later you will need this timeline for insurers, customers, your own review and possibly the regulator. Memory under stress is unreliable.

## Minute 0 to 15: confirm and gather the right people

- **Confirm it is real.** Is the strange email actually from your system? Are files actually encrypted or just a sync issue? Spend a few minutes checking, not an hour.
- **Name an incident lead.** One person makes decisions and keeps the log. In a small business this is usually a director or whoever manages IT.
- **Call for help early.** Contact your IT provider or security partner now, not after you have tried to fix it yourself. If you have cyber insurance, check the policy: many insurers require you to call their incident line first, and acting before you do can affect your cover.
- **Use a safe channel.** If email or a messaging account may be compromised, do not discuss the incident on it. Use phone calls or a separate channel.

## Minute 15 to 45: contain the damage

Containment means stopping the incident from spreading, without destroying evidence.

**If an account is compromised:**
- Reset the password from a clean device and sign out all active sessions.
- Turn on two-factor authentication if it was not already on.
- Check for forwarding rules or inbox rules the attacker may have added to hide replies or copy mail.
- Warn colleagues and, if fake messages went out, the customers who received them.

**If ransomware or malware is suspected:**
- **Disconnect affected devices from the network**: unplug the cable and turn off Wi-Fi.
- **Do not switch them off** unless your IT provider tells you to. Memory can hold useful evidence.
- Disconnect or pause backup systems so they are not encrypted too.
- Check whether other devices show the same signs.

**If data may have leaked:**
- Work out what data, whose data, and how much, as far as you can.
- Remove public access to anything exposed, such as a shared link or a misconfigured storage folder.

## Minute 45 to 60: assess severity and plan the next steps

Decide how serious the incident is. A simple, practical rule: severity is set by the more serious of two things, **the type of data involved** and **the impact on services**. Personal or financial data exposed, or a total outage of a core system, puts an incident at the highest level.

Then decide:

- who needs to know internally, and what you will tell staff
- whether customers or partners need to be told now
- whether you have legal or contractual duties to notify anyone

Notification duties depend on your country, sector and contracts. In Sri Lanka, the Personal Data Protection Act places obligations on businesses when personal data is affected. Our [plain-English guide to the PDPA](/blog/sri-lanka-personal-data-protection-act-guide-for-small-businesses) explains the basics, but get qualified advice on what applies to your situation.

## What not to do

- **Do not wipe and rebuild immediately.** It feels decisive but destroys the evidence you need to find out how the attacker got in, and they may get in again the same way.
- **Do not pay a ransom on impulse.** Payment does not guarantee recovery, may have legal implications, and should only ever be considered with professional and legal advice.
- **Do not contact the attacker** yourself.
- **Do not hide it.** Staff who fear blame stay quiet, and incidents that are reported late are always worse.
- **Do not announce details publicly** before you understand what happened.

## After the first hour

Once the incident is contained, recovery begins: restoring from clean backups, closing the weakness that let the attacker in, and checking that nothing was left behind. When it is over, hold a short **post-incident review** with everyone involved:

- What happened, in order, using your log.
- What went well.
- What the root cause was.
- What you will change, with owners and dates.

The review is where an incident becomes an improvement. Keep it factual and blame-free, or people will not be honest next time.

## Prepare now, in one afternoon

The first hour goes far better when decisions were made in advance. Before anything happens:

1. **Write down your roles:** who leads, who contacts the IT provider, who talks to customers.
2. **Keep a contact list offline:** IT provider, insurer's incident line, bank, key suppliers, a lawyer.
3. **Define severity levels** so you are not debating them under pressure.
4. **Print the first-hour checklist** and store it where people can find it.
5. **Run a 45-minute walk-through** once a year. Pick a scenario, such as "the finance manager's email is taken over", and talk through what each person would do. You will find gaps in minutes.
