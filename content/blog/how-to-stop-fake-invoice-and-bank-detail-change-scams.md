One of the most costly scams a small business can fall for does not involve any malware at all. An email arrives that appears to come from a regular supplier. It says their bank account has changed and asks you to pay the next invoice into a new account. The invoice looks right, the amount is right and the timing is right. You pay it. A week later, the real supplier asks why they have not been paid.

This is known as payment diversion, invoice fraud or business email compromise. It works because it targets a normal business process, not a technical weakness. The good news is that a simple, consistently applied verification process stops almost all of it.

## How these scams work

There are a few common versions:

- **A compromised supplier mailbox.** Criminals break into a supplier's email account, read real conversations and then send a genuine-looking message from the real address, often replying in an existing thread.
- **A lookalike email address.** The message comes from an address that is very close to the real one, such as a single letter changed or a different domain ending.
- **A compromised mailbox in your own business.** Criminals get into one of your staff's accounts and send payment instructions to customers or ask your finance team to pay a "new supplier".
- **The impersonated boss.** A message appears to come from the owner or a director, asking for an urgent payment to be made quietly.

In each case, the criminal often sets up mailbox rules that hide replies, so the real person does not notice the conversation happening.

## The warning signs

Train everyone who handles payments to pause when they see any of these:

- a request to change bank details, especially by email alone
- urgency or pressure: "pay today", "the account will be frozen", "the director needs this before the meeting"
- a request for secrecy or to bypass the normal process
- a new account in a different name, bank or country from usual
- small changes in the sender's address, signature, tone or writing style
- a reply-to address that differs from the sender's address
- attachments or links that ask you to sign in to view an invoice

None of these prove fraud on their own, but each one is a reason to verify before paying.

## Step 1: Make verification a rule, not a judgement call

The single most effective control is a written rule that everyone follows, every time:

> Any new payee, or any change to an existing payee's bank details, must be verified by phone using a number we already hold on record, before any payment is made.

Two details make this work:

- **Use a number you already have**, from your supplier file, a previous contract or the supplier's official website. Never use the phone number in the email that asked for the change, because it may belong to the criminal.
- **Speak to someone you know** at the supplier, and ask them to confirm the change. Do not read the new details out to them; ask them to tell you the account details instead.

Record the check: who called, who they spoke to, the date and the outcome. This takes two minutes and is good evidence for auditors and insurers.

## Step 2: Separate duties for payments

Even in a small business, avoid one person being able to add a new payee and send money alone. Common approaches:

- one person sets up or changes payee details, another approves the first payment
- payments above a set amount need a second approver in online banking
- the owner or a director reviews new payees each week

Many banks offer dual authorisation in their business online banking. Ask your bank what controls they provide for business accounts and payee changes.

## Step 3: Protect your own email

Many of these scams start with a compromised mailbox. Basic email security makes your business both a harder target and a less useful one for impersonating you to your customers:

- **Two-factor authentication** on every email account, especially finance and management.
- **A password manager** so each account has a unique password. Our guide to [setting up a password manager](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-password-manager-for-a-small-team) walks through it.
- **Alerts for new forwarding rules and suspicious sign-ins**, which Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 both offer through their admin tools.
- **Email authentication records** on your domain, so criminals find it harder to send email that appears to come from your exact address.

Our [website security checklist](/blog/website-security-checklist-for-small-businesses) covers the domain and account basics in more detail.

## Step 4: Tell your customers how you will and will not ask for payment

Criminals impersonate you to your customers as well. Protect them, and your reputation, by stating clearly how you handle payments:

- add a line to invoices and your email signature such as: "We will never change our bank details by email. If you receive a request like this, call us on [number] before paying."
- tell regular customers directly if you ever genuinely change bank accounts, by phone as well as in writing
- put your official contact details on your website so customers can check them

## Step 5: Train the people who handle money

A short, practical briefing is enough. Cover:

- how the scam works, using a fictional example
- the warning signs above
- the verification rule and how to record checks
- that nobody, including the owner, will be criticised for delaying a payment to verify it

That last point matters. Scams succeed by applying pressure. Staff need to know they are expected to verify, even when a "director" says it is urgent.

## What to do if you think you have paid a criminal

Speed matters. The sooner the bank knows, the better the chance of stopping or recovering funds, although recovery is not guaranteed.

1. **Call your bank immediately** using the number on the back of your card or the bank's official website, and report a fraudulent payment. Ask them to contact the receiving bank.
2. **Tell the real supplier or customer** so they can check their own systems.
3. **Secure your email.** Change passwords, sign out all sessions, check for forwarding rules and review recent sign-ins.
4. **Report it.** In Sri Lanka, you can report to the police, and Sri Lanka CERT (cert.gov.lk) handles cyber security incident reports. Check their websites for current reporting channels.
5. **Keep evidence.** Save the emails with full headers, invoices and payment records. Do not delete anything.
6. **Consider personal data.** If a mailbox was compromised, personal data may have been exposed. Sri Lanka's Personal Data Protection Act No. 9 of 2022 includes obligations around personal data breaches. This is general information, not legal advice, so check the Act and guidance from the Data Protection Authority of Sri Lanka.

A written incident response plan makes these first steps much faster under pressure.

## A one-page checklist for your finance team

- New payee or changed bank details? Call back on a known number before paying.
- Urgent, secret or unusual request? Stop and verify with the person directly.
- Record every verification check.
- Two people involved in any new payee and its first payment.
- Report anything suspicious to the owner or IT contact straight away.

Print it and keep it next to whoever pays the bills. These scams depend on one person acting quickly and alone. A two-minute phone call breaks that pattern.
